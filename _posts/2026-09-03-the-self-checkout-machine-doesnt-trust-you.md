Every self-checkout lane treats you like a suspect before you've done anything wrong. The scale under the bagging area watches your every move, a camera above your head records the whole transaction, and the second you set something down oddly a light starts flashing and a voice tells you to wait for assistance. I didn't sign up to be surveilled. I signed up to buy groceries.

And that's when the machine actually works. Half the time it doesn't: a barcode won't scan, the produce screen makes you hunt through forty varieties to find "grapes, red, seedless", the card reader freezes mid-transaction. When a cashier's till jams, that's the store's problem. When a self-checkout kiosk jams, it's yours, and you're the one troubleshooting a machine you didn't build, for free.

A cashier used to be a small, ordinary human exchange, thirty seconds of "how's your day going" that cost nothing and asked nothing of you. Self-checkout replaces that with a transaction between a person and a machine that's actively suspicious of them, and people have noticed. A [study covered by CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2024/01/23/business/self-checkout-shopping-stores) found shoppers souring on self-checkout specifically because it feels worse, not because it's slower, and some retailers, Walmart included, are already scaling it back.

I don't think every interaction needs to be efficient. Some of them are just supposed to be pleasant, and we traded that away for a machine that beeps at us and calls it progress.
