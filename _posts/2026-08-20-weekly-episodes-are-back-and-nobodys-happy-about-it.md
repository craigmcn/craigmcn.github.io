Netflix built its identity on dropping a full season at midnight and letting you disappear into it for a weekend. Now Netflix, Disney+, HBO and Apple TV+ are all walking that back, releasing episodes one at a time again like it's 1998. Nobody asked for this. The streamers just decided the thing they trained a whole generation to expect wasn't good for business, and are quietly undoing it while calling it innovation.

Strip away the app icons and what you're left with is the exact scheduling logic of network television thirty years ago, rebuilt at enormous expense. The official line is that weekly drops create "watercooler conversation," a phrase executives use with real fondness, as though restoring something culturally precious. They aren't. [Weekly releases keep subscribers paying for longer](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-news/weekly-releases-vs-binge-whats-better-for-streamers-1236403891/) instead of bingeing a season in two days and cancelling before the next bill. The nostalgia is real enough, but it's a retention metric wearing a costume.

I'm part of the problem they're solving for. I wait until two or three series I actually want have finished airing, then subscribe just long enough to burn through all of them and cancel again. It's not loyalty, it's a raid. But it isn't unreasonable either, because the real driver is arithmetic, not laziness. There are too many services now for anyone to hold five or six at once and still call it a budget. So people ration, rotating a subscription or two instead of paying for everything year-round, and weekly drops exist largely to squeeze a few more billing cycles out of each raid.

Here's what the executives seem to have missed. Slowing my binge doesn't make me subscribe to more services at once, because my budget hasn't grown to match their catalogues. All it does is push my start date later, since I'll wait even longer for a season to finish before signing up. Cord-cutting was sold as freedom from the schedule, and here we are, checking release calendars again, except now there are five of them instead of one, and the fight over who controls my calendar just moved out into the open.
